How high should the top tax rate be?

Every income tax ends in a top bracket, and the rate on that bracket starts more political fights than any other number in the tax code.

Saez shows that the best top rate follows from three numbers you can measure: how much income sits far above the top line, how much top earners shrink their income when taxed, and how much society values their last dollar.

By the end you can read his formula, say which of the three numbers each side of the debate is assuming, and say which parts of the argument you should doubt.

1 / 8 · Three effects

So far: the question is where to set the top step.

Raise the top rate a little, and three things happen.

Saez does not search over every possible rate. He asks a smaller question instead: what happens if we raise the current top rate by one small step?

The government gains, because top earners pay more on the same income. Economists call this the mechanical effect.

The government also loses, because some top earners respond by reporting less income, so there is less to tax. This is the behavioural effect.

Top earners lose too, because they keep less: the welfare effect. It counts only as much as society values their dollars.

If the gain beats the two losses, raise the rate; if not, lower it. The best rate is where they balance, so the question becomes how large each loss is.

Why a small step? At the best rate a small change in either direction makes no difference, so finding where small changes stop paying off finds the best rate. Economists call this a first-order condition.
2 / 8 · The gain

So far: a small rate rise brings one gain and two losses.

The gain depends on how much income sits far above the line.

The top rate applies only to income above a line, \(\bar z\). When the rate rises, each top earner pays more on their slice above that line, \(z_m - \bar z\), where \(z_m\) is their average income.

That makes the size of the slice the whole story for the gain, and the slice depends on the shape of the top of the income distribution.

Top incomes follow a Pareto tail, a shape set by one number, \(a\). Above any line, the average income is

\[ z_m = \frac{a}{a-1}\,\bar z \]

\(z_m\)
average income above the line
\(a\)
Pareto tail; small means fat
\(\bar z\)
the line where the top rate starts

so a small \(a\) means a fat tail, with many very high incomes, a big slice and a big gain. Move the slider to see it.

Side note. Vilfredo Pareto found this shape in Italian land records in the 1890s, and US tax data shows it too, with \(a\) near 1.5. Screen 5 uses one consequence: \((z_m-\bar z)/z_m = 1/a\) exactly.
3 / 8 · The loss

So far: the gain is the slice above the line, and a fat tail makes it bigger.

The loss depends on how much reported income shrinks.

A higher rate leaves top earners a smaller take-home share, \(1-\tau\). Some of them respond: they work less, take pay in other forms, or avoid tax.

The elasticity of taxable income, \(e\), measures that response. If the take-home share falls by 10%, reported income falls by \(e \times 10\%\).

Every lost dollar of income costs the government \(\tau\) in tax, and that is the behavioural loss.

Estimates of \(e\) run from about 0.1 to above 1, which is why most of the political fight over top rates is really a fight over this one number.

4 / 8 · Where they meet

So far: the gain comes from the tail, \(a\), and the loss comes from the response, \(e\).

The best rate is where the gain and the loss meet.

The flat line is the gain from one small step up, and it is the same at every rate. Its height is \((1-\bar g)/a\): the slice from screen 2, minus the part society counts as a loss to top earners.

The curve is the behavioural loss. It stays small at low rates and climbs fast toward 100%, because each lost dollar costs more tax when \(\tau\) is high.

Left of the crossing, a step up gains more than it loses, so the rate should rise. Right of it, the rate should fall. The crossing itself is \(\tau^*\), where a small step changes nothing (first-order condition).

About \(\bar g\). It is the value society puts on one more dollar for a top earner, compared with a dollar of public money. With \(\bar g = 0\), the best rate is the one that raises the most revenue.

6 / 8 · What to doubt

So far: the formula follows cleanly from the model. Now ask what the model takes for granted.

The formula is proved; the inputs are where the doubt lives.

The algebra on screen 5 holds whenever the model's assumptions hold, so the useful question is which assumptions could fail. The answer matters most for \(e\), because the rate is so sensitive to it.

The model treats \(e\) as a fixed feature of top earners, but much of their response is avoidance, and avoidance depends on how the tax is designed. If a government closes loopholes, the same people respond less, so \(e\) is partly a policy choice rather than a fact of nature.

The weight \(\bar g\) is different again. No data set measures it, because it is a value judgment about how much a rich person's dollar matters.

The result also only applies within its scope: a top bracket with a Pareto shape, one tax base, and no movement of people or income across borders.

8 / 8 · Check yourself

So far: the whole argument and its weak points. Now make it stick.

Answer before you reveal.

Top earners stop responding to taxes, so \(e = 0\). Where does the curve on screen 4 go, and what is \(\tau^*\)?

The loss curve lies flat on zero, so it never meets the gain line before 100%. With \(\bar g = 0\), \(\tau^*\) is 100%, because raising the rate never loses revenue.

Two economists agree on \(a\) and \(\bar g\) but recommend 45% and 75%. What do they disagree about?

They disagree about \(e\). The economist at 45% believes top earners respond strongly, and the one at 75% believes they respond little, because \(e\) is the only remaining input.

Which input to the formula is a value judgment rather than a measurement?

The social weight \(\bar g\), because no data can say how much a rich person's extra dollar should matter to society. That is why screen 6 tags it as interpretation.

Marginal tax rate

The share of your next dollar of income that goes in tax.

In plain words. Income is taxed in brackets. The marginal rate is the rate on the bracket your last dollar falls in, not the share of all your income paid in tax.

Example. A single filer earning $700k in 2024 pays 37% on each dollar above $609,350, but much less on the dollars below it.

Don't confuse with. The average rate: total tax divided by total income, which is always lower for a top earner.

Top tax rate

The marginal rate on income above the top line; \(\tau^*\) is the best one.

In plain words. The rate on the highest bracket. Saez asks what value of it is best, and calls the answer \(\tau^*\).

Example. The US top rate in 2024 is 37%. With \(e = 0.25\), \(a = 1.5\) and \(\bar g = 0\), the formula gives \(\tau^* = 73\%\).

Symbol. \(\tau\) is the Greek letter tau, a number between 0 and 1.

Top line

The income where the top bracket starts.

In plain words. Only income above this line is taxed at the top rate, so only that slice matters for the argument.

Example. $609,350 for a single US filer in 2024; the figures round it to $600k.

Symbol. \(\bar z\), read "z bar". \(z\) stands for income throughout the paper.

Average income above the line

The mean income of everyone who earns more than the top line.

In plain words. Take all top earners and average their incomes. The gap \(z_m - \bar z\) is the slice the top rate actually taxes for a typical top earner.

Example. With \(a = 1.5\) and a $600k line, \(z_m = 3 \times 600\text{k} = 1.8\text{M}\), so the taxed slice is $1.2M.

Symbol. \(z_m\), read "z sub m", for mean.

Pareto tail

The shape of top incomes, set by one number \(a\): smaller means more very high incomes.

In plain words. Above any high line, incomes thin out at the same proportional rate. That makes the average above the line a fixed multiple of the line: \(z_m = \frac{a}{a-1}\bar z\).

Example. \(a = 1.5\) gives a multiple of 3; \(a = 3\) gives only 1.5. A fatter tail (small \(a\)) means a bigger slice to tax.

Don't confuse with. The Pareto principle ("80/20") and Pareto efficiency, which are different ideas from the same economist.

Elasticity of taxable income

How much reported income shrinks when take-home pay per dollar falls.

In plain words. It is a ratio of percentages: the percent fall in reported income for each 1% fall in the take-home share \(1 - \tau\).

Example. With \(e = 0.25\), cutting the take-home share by 10% cuts reported income by 2.5%: $45k for a $1.8M earner.

Don't confuse with. Labour supply. Reported income also falls through avoidance and timing, so \(e\) partly reflects how the tax is designed.

Social welfare weight

How much society values one more dollar for a top earner, in public-money terms.

In plain words. A value judgment, not a measurement. \(\bar g = 0\) says a top earner's extra dollar adds nothing society cares about; \(\bar g = 1\) says it counts the same as a dollar of public money.

Example. Raising \(\bar g\) from 0 to 0.5 lowers the gain line on screen 4 by half, and the best rate falls from 73% to 57%.

Symbol. \(\bar g\), read "g bar", the average weight across top earners.

Mechanical effect

The extra tax raised if nobody changed their behaviour.

In plain words. Raise the rate and apply it to the same incomes as before. The extra revenue is the mechanical effect, the only gain from a rate rise.

Example. One extra point on a $1.2M taxed slice raises $12k per top earner.

Don't confuse with. The total change in revenue, which also subtracts the behavioural effect.

Behavioural effect

The tax lost because people report less income after the rate rises.

In plain words. Some top earners respond to a smaller take-home share by working, earning or reporting less. Every dollar they no longer report costs the government \(\tau\) in tax.

Example. Screen 3: a 37% to 43% rise with \(e = 0.25\) costs about $19.5k per top earner.

Don't confuse with. The welfare effect, which is about how top earners feel, not about revenue.

Welfare effect

The loss to top earners from keeping less, counted at the weight \(\bar g\).

In plain words. The mechanical gain is money taken from top earners. How much that matters to society depends on \(\bar g\); at \(\bar g = 0\) it does not count at all.

Example. With \(\bar g = 0.3\), a $12k mechanical gain carries a welfare cost of $3.6k, so the net gain is $8.4k.

First-order condition

At the best value, a small change in either direction gains nothing.

In plain words. If a small step up still helped, you would take it; if it hurt, you would step down. So at the best rate, gains and losses from a small step exactly cancel.

Example. On screen 4 that is the crossing: left of it a step up helps, right of it a step down helps.

Don't confuse with. A proof that the point is the best overall; the condition finds candidates, and the shape of the curves confirms it.
